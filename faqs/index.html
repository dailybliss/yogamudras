The Basics

What is a mudra?

A mudra is a symbolic hand gesture used in yoga and meditation. The word joins the Sanskrit mud (joy, delight) and ra (to draw forth) — that which draws forth joy. Each gesture positions the fingers to balance the body's five elements. Browse them all in the mudra library.

Who discovered mudras?

Nobody knows for certain, and that mystery is part of what makes the practice interesting. Hand gestures of this kind predate written record, with the earliest surviving references appearing in classical Hindu texts on chanting, worship and dance. The Bhagavad Gita itself pictures Krishna holding Gyan Mudra as he teaches Arjuna, and similar gestures mark the traditional iconography of Mahavir in Jainism and Guru Nanak in Sikhism. Some accounts trace mudras further back still, to Shiva's cosmic dance, the Ananda Tandava.

The honest answer is that mudras were likely never invented at a single moment by a single person — they were refined gradually, across many centuries and traditions, which is part of why the same gesture can carry slightly different meanings depending on where you encounter it. Read more on the history & origins page.

How long should I hold a mudra?

Most hand mudras are held for ten to fifteen minutes. Some, like Hakini Mudra, work in as little as two to five minutes, while deeper practices such as Vayu Mudra suit longer sessions. There's no strict universal ceiling, though very basic mudras are generally suggested for up to forty-five minutes a day as a sensible guide. The more important principle is comfort — follow the duration given for whichever mudra you're practising, and stop if anything feels uncomfortable.

Can children practise mudras?

Yes, comfortably. Mudras ask for no strength, flexibility or equipment, so a school-going child can learn one as easily as an adult. Gyan Mudra is a natural starting point — it's simple to form, has a calming effect, and is traditionally associated with focus and memory, which makes it a pleasant gesture to introduce alongside a breathing exercise. Anjali Mudra, the palms-together gesture used in "namaste," is equally gentle and easy for a child to hold.

Do I need any yoga experience to begin?

No. Mudras are practised seated, using only the hands — no prior yoga experience, flexibility or fitness is required, and a chair works perfectly well. Start with the practice guidelines for the handful of principles worth knowing before your first session.

finding the right gesture

Choosing a Mudra

Which mudra is best for meditation?

Gyan Mudra is the gesture most people picture first — thumb and index finger meeting, palms resting on the knees. It's traditionally linked to sharper concentration and memory, which makes it a natural choice whenever the goal is mental clarity. Dhyan Mudra — hands resting one inside the other in the lap — appears across Buddhist and yogic meditation alike and is associated with a deeper, more settled state of stillness rather than active focus.

Which one to choose really depends on what you're after in that sitting: Gyan Mudra for clarity and attention, Dhyan Mudra for depth and quiet.

Which mudra is best for kids?

Gyan Mudra and Anjali Mudra are the two gentlest starting points — both simple to form, calming on the nervous system, and easy for a child to hold without strain. Anjali Mudra in particular is already familiar to most children through the "namaste" gesture used at the start and end of a yoga class, which makes it an easy first mudra to introduce.

Which mudra is best for women?

Gyan Mudra supports mental clarity in the same way for anyone, while Prana Mudra is traditionally considered one of the more significant gestures for activating the body's stored energy — useful when fatigue or low vitality is the concern. Apan Mudra is worth knowing about too — among its traditional associations is support for regulating the menstrual cycle. Padma Mudra, the lotus gesture, is linked to the goddess Lakshmi and traditionally associated with grace, compassion and abundance.

There are also mudras specifically associated with feminine energy and hormonal balance — Yoni Mudra, Trimurti Mudra and Kali Mudra among them — each carrying its own history and intended use.

Which mudra is best for people over 60?

Garuda Mudra is traditionally said to improve balance and coordination, which can be particularly useful at this stage of life. Prana Mudra is considered one of the more significant gestures for activating the body's stored energy, traditionally linked to better circulation, heart health and steadier energy levels. Aditi Mudra is traditionally associated with mental clarity and focus, Ganesh Mudra is said to help clear obstacles and support success, and Surabhi Mudra is traditionally used to balance the body's five elements — earth, water, fire, air and space — for overall wellbeing.

Which mudra helps with skin health?

Varun Mudra — named for the water element — is the most direct answer here, traditionally used to support hydration and reduce skin dryness over time. Apan Mudra works from a different angle: alongside supporting the body's elimination process, it's traditionally said to give the skin a healthy glow. Prithvi Mudra, the earth-element gesture, is traditionally associated with skin, hair and nail health.

A few more targeted gestures — Brahma Mudra, Kaki Mudra and Shanmukhi Mudra — each address skin health through a different mechanism, from circulation to anti-ageing to reducing pigmentation.

getting it right

Practice & Timing

Can I practise more than one mudra in a day?

For the more advanced mudras especially, it's best to keep to a single gesture per day while you're building the habit. Once you're comfortable, working with up to two in a day is generally fine — more than that tends to dilute the focus each one is meant to bring.

Can I hold a mudra for more than 45 minutes?

Each mudra has its own recommended duration, and that's the range worth following rather than a single number that applies to everything. For very basic mudras, around forty-five minutes a day is often suggested as a general daily maximum — not a strict rule tied to any specific risk. There are no documented side effects from mudra practice. Sticking to the guideline for whichever mudra you're practising simply lowers the chance of temporary discomfort and tends to bring better results over time.

Beyond the guidelines, the best measure is your own body. Practise at a level that's comfortable, pay attention to how you feel, and stop if a mudra ever feels uncomfortable to hold.

Can mudras be practised at night?

Daytime is best, and early morning especially so. Evening practice is fine if that's what fits your schedule — but a few specific mudras are the exception. Surya Mudra, for instance, activates the body's fire element and is traditionally avoided at night, since it can work against the body's natural wind-down.

Should I eat before or after practising?

If you've just had a meal, it's best to wait at least an hour before practising. Once you're done, wait around thirty minutes before eating again. If you're pressed for time, waiting at least fifteen minutes after practice before eating is an acceptable minimum.

What is the best time of day to practise mudras?

Traditionally, brahma muhurta — the hour or so before sunrise — is considered the most receptive window for practice, followed by the broader morning stretch between 6 and 8am, and evenings between 4 and 8pm.

One thing worth knowing: brahma muhurta isn't a fixed clock time. It shifts daily with the local sunrise, which makes it a little fiddly to track by hand.

Can I extend a mudra beyond its recommended duration?

It's best to stick to the range given for whichever mudra you're practising. Each recommended duration reflects how that gesture is meant to work, and there's no evidence that holding it longer deepens the benefit — if anything, following the guideline tends to bring better results and lowers the chance of temporary discomfort. As with any practice, comfort comes first: if something feels off, ease out of the mudra rather than pushing through.
