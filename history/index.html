The History of Mudras

From Vedic ritual to the Buddha's hands, from temple dance to today's meditation cushions — how a language of gesture travelled through thousands of years.

What the word means

Mudras hold an important place in the cultures of the East, and their story is as varied as the traditions that carry them. The word itself comes from Sanskrit, where it is most often translated as a seal, a sign or a mark — a gesture that stamps a particular meaning or intention.

Mudra — seal, signThe everyday Sanskrit sense: a gesture that seals in an intention, like a stamp leaves its mark.
Mud + ra — to draw forth joyA traditional reading of the word's roots: mud, joy or delight, and ra, to draw forth.

A journey through time

No one can point to a single moment when mudras began. Their origins remain something of a mystery, but their path through history can be traced through scripture, art and practice.

  1. Before written record

    Possible early roots

    Some scholars see hints of ritual hand positions in figures on seals from the Indus Valley civilisation. Their meaning is uncertain, but they suggest the idea of sacred gesture may be very old indeed.

  2. c. 1500 – 500 BCE

    The Vedic period

    The earliest references to mudras are traced to the Vedas, India's ancient scriptures. Here, symbolic gestures accompanied chanting and ritual, giving physical form to sacred sound and intention.

  3. 6th – 5th century BCE

    The age of the Buddha

    Around the time of Gautama Buddha, mudras became woven into meditation and yogic practice. The Buddha is very often shown holding a mudra, and each one carries its own message — while also being understood to guide energy towards the brain and influence the subtle energies of the body.

  4. Classical era

    Scripture, worship and dance

    Mudras appear across classical Hindu texts on chanting (Mantra Shastra), worship (Upasana Shastra) and dance (Nritya Shastra). Krishna is traditionally depicted holding Gyan Mudra as he teaches the Bhagavad Gita to Arjuna.

  5. 15th – 17th century

    Hatha yoga texts

    Hatha yoga manuals such as the Hatha Yoga Pradipika and the Gheranda Samhita set out mudras as a formal part of yogic practice, alongside postures, breathwork and meditation.

  6. Today

    A global practice

    In recent decades, mudras have travelled far beyond India. People around the world now bring them into meditation and everyday wellness routines — an ancient practice that has kept its relevance in modern life.

One gesture, many traditions

Mudras belong to no single faith or art. The same hand positions recur across religions, sculpture and performance, each tradition giving them its own emphasis.

Hinduism

Deities are shown with mudras that express their qualities, and some accounts trace mudras to Lord Shiva himself — several are said to come from his cosmic dance, the Ananda Tandava.

Buddhism

Statues of the Buddha use mudras as a visual language — for teaching, meditation, fearlessness and generosity. As Buddhism spread across Asia, these gestures travelled with it.

Jainism & Sikhism

Mahavir in Jainism and Guru Nanak in Sikhism are often depicted holding Gyan or Dhyan Mudra — gestures of wisdom and deep meditation.

Classical dance

In Indian classical dance, mudras are a core part of the dancer's vocabulary. Each gesture conveys a particular emotion or part of a story, adding depth and meaning to the performance.

Mudras in yoga

Within yoga, mudras serve a more inward purpose. They are used to support the flow of energy through the body and to deepen the journey within during meditation.

Practising a mudra is also seen as a way of attuning yourself to particular aspects of the universe — each gesture creating a kind of resonance with a specific frequency of energy. To understand how this is said to work, finger by finger, see the science of mudras.

A gesture that has outlived empires, and still fits in the palm of your hand.

Why mudras endure

The roots of mudras run deep into the ancient philosophies and practices of the East. That they still appear in worship, art, dance and modern wellness speaks to their lasting importance — and to the universal appeal of what they symbolise. Thousands of years on, they continue to offer a simple bridge between the physical and the spiritual.
